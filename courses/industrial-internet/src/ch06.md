# Chapter 6 · One Purpose

The shield is up. For the first time since the Raspberry Pi, nothing is on fire, and there's time to look up from the wiring. The moment we do, a question we dodged for five chapters walks back in. It was there at the very first meeting, when procurement asked "does your machine support the industrial Internet?", we said "it has a 4G module," and he said *that doesn't count*. We built sensors, networks, identifiers, a platform, a shield — and never once stopped to name the thing. This last chapter does that. It isn't new material; it's the same five chapters, seen from thirty thousand feet.

## What Do We Call It?

The word "industrial Internet" (工业互联网) means different things to different people, because it's three things wearing one coat. The definition you'll meet in the course: the **new infrastructure, application model, and industrial ecosystem** (新型基础设施、应用模式、工业生态) born from the deep fusion of new-generation ICT and the industrial economy. Three nouns, and each has a different owner:

| The word | Who actually does it | What it looks like on the ground |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New infrastructure (新型基础设施) | Government and carriers | National top-level nodes, hundreds of secondary nodes, 5G private networks |
| Application model (应用模式) | Platform vendors | A machine-tool maker wiring its CNC machines to a platform for remote service |
| Industrial ecosystem (工业生态) | Industry alliances | IIC (2014, GE/AT&T/Cisco/IBM/Intel), AII (2016, thousands of members) |

That's why the word sounds like a slogan: it's addressed to three audiences at once. In practice it's a compass — whenever someone says "industrial Internet," first ask which side they're standing on. Government talks nodes and private networks; a platform vendor talks subscriptions and models; an alliance talks standards and mutual recognition. We've been doing the middle one all along, but we leaned on the first one's infrastructure and the third one's interfaces.

There's an older, more concrete phrasing, from GE, who coined the concept in 2012: the industrial Internet is an **open, global network that connects people, data, and machines**. That's the technical description; the three-noun version is the economic position. Both are true; they just point at the two forces that created this market — equipment makers wanting remote service, and a country wanting infrastructure.

## Why We Did It

Strip away the jargon and the reason is a line in a spreadsheet. We sell a machine once and make 81,000 yuan (450,000 × 18% margin). We service it for years and make 16,200 yuan a year, indefinitely, at 45% margin. Three-tenths of the installed base renewing their service contract is **3.89 million yuan a year — the profit of 48 more machines, with no materials spent and no production capacity occupied**. That's the whole point of the industrial Internet for an equipment maker: it turns a one-time sale into a recurring service.

But there's a catch, and it's the catch that shaped every decision in this book. Service revenue only exists if a *third party* admits your machine was available. Your own number doesn't count; the customer's number you don't trust; and the two have to reconcile. This is the constraint hiding under "mutually agreed" on that first sheet — **data has to be believable to someone else**. The sheet's four lines were never four separate requests; they were four faces of one demand: make the machine's life *legible to an outsider*.

And notice how deep that demand runs: it crosses devices, systems, plants, and regions (跨设备、跨系统、跨厂区、跨地区). Four crossings, and each one is a wall the data must pass through with its meaning intact. That's why a Raspberry Pi and some tape could never have worked — the problem was never "get some numbers," it was "get numbers that survive the journey and still mean the same thing at the other end."

## Someone Already Paid the Tuition

Before we spend another year, look at the company that tried the ambitious version and died. Shenyang Machine Tool's i5 system packed industrialization, informatization, networking, intelligence, and integration into one controller, with metering sensors built into the machine. The business model even reached "zero yuan down, pay per hour of spindle time" and revenue-sharing between maker, supplier, and customer. It was five to eight years ahead of everyone.

It ended in bankruptcy restructuring — not because the technology was wrong, but because the money ran out before the customers agreed. From an engineer's chair, that failure leaves three rules sharper than any definition:

1. **Metering is not billing.** You can count 3,200 hours of use; the customer can simply refuse your count.
2. **Connected is not permitted.** The data is born in the customer's workshop, and it belongs to them.
3. **Data in a contract becomes evidence.** Get it wrong and you pay; and you can pay more than the service was worth.

That's why our first-year goal was deliberately low: not hourly billing, not predictive maintenance — just **one report the customer would sign**. Everything in this book served that one unglamorous goal. The order, the blueprint, the thread, the service, the shield — all of it existed to produce a number a customer would put their name on.

## Three Lines in the Sand

When someone asks how this differs from the internet, the honest answer fits one sentence: *when the internet breaks, you refresh the page; when our machine breaks, we pay 5,000 yuan an hour*. That one difference turns "low latency, high reliability, wide coverage" from marketing words into contract clauses — latency needs an upper bound, availability goes into the contract, and every fault must trace to a person. Consumer internet can refuse those promises; we can't, because the promise is priced.

Against the **Internet of Things** (物联网), the difference is a single word: **closure**. IoT's six features aren't six adjectives, they're a chain — intelligent perception → ubiquitous connectivity → digital modeling → real-time analysis → **precise control** → iterative optimization. The watershed is the fifth link: *control*. Consumer IoT only needs to see; industry needs to close the loop and act. A failed sensor in a consumer gadget means you swap it; a failed sensor that misses a broken tool ruins a whole batch. It's also why a lot of "predictive maintenance" stalls at the fourth link — sensing, connectivity, analysis, and prediction all work, but the fifth step means writing an instruction that changes the customer's machining parameters, and nobody will sign the liability for that.

Against **smart manufacturing** (智能制造), the relationship is: the industrial Internet is its *foundation*, and smart manufacturing is its *destination*. Said on the floor: no customer pays for a goal called "smart manufacturing"; they pay for the four requirements. But delivering those four requirements *is* the whole point — the shift from a manufacturer to a service company.

## The Budget Tells the Truth

One more way to see the whole thing, and it's the way the money sees it. The course says network is the foundation, platform is the core, security is the guarantee. Put that on a budget and watch it move:

| | Network | Platform | Security |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Year one, winning the order | 500,000 | 300,000 | 0 |
| Year three, under contract | 250,000 | 300,000 | 250,000 |

Security was zero and became a quarter of the budget. Not because we got wiser — because we signed a contract. The moment a contract names data ownership and liability, security stops being optional and becomes an entry ticket. That's what "security is the guarantee" really means: **it produces no revenue; it decides whether you're allowed to collect any.** The three aren't just blocks on an architecture diagram; they're line items, and their ratio is a function of how much you've promised.

## Wrapping Up

Five chapters, one breath: an order made the machine's life legible to a customer; a blueprint said what the whole was made of; a thread gave every thing one identity and a way to be found; a service turned the flood of data into something worth paying for; a shield kept all those open doors from being used against us. And the thing all of it was for — the thing we finally have a name for — is the industrial Internet: not a technology you install, but a way of making a machine's data *believable*, so that a one-time sale becomes a service someone renews.

That's the purpose. The 4G module the procurement guy dismissed wasn't wrong because it was too little technology; it was wrong because it answered "can it connect" when he was asking "will its data be trustworthy enough to build a business on." We spent five chapters learning the difference.
