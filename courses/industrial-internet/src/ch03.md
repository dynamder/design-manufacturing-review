# Chapter 3 · One Thread

The blueprint is on the wall: network is the foundation, platform is the core, security is the guarantee — and "how each is actually built" was handed to the chapters to come. The network is ours. We can't build the whole foundation at once, so we start with the one job the customer is literally waiting on: that part from Chapter 2. Three names, one part. The blueprint said "identifier resolution." Let's actually do it.

## The Sticker That Can't Answer Back

You start the way any engineer would: print a number on the part. A barcode (条形码), maybe a QR code. Stamp it, stick it, done. Cheap, instant, and the part now has one label.

It works — inside your own factory. The moment you scan it, you know it's `86.10000/ac.qd.1024`.

But watch what happens when the part leaves. The customer's site is eight hundred kilometers away, and nobody there walks around with a scanner. The label gets greased, scraped, buried in swarf. And even when someone does scan it, you get a *number* and nothing else. Which database holds its birth certificate? Which system knows its batch? The number doesn't say. You've given the thing a name, but no way to look it up.

That's the difference between a domain name and an identifier. When you type `www.baidu.com`, the domain name system (DNS) turns it into an IP address and stops. When you resolve an identifier like `86.10000/ac.qd.1024`, the system must return the address *and* pull out the business information bound to that identity — machine, batch, tool, history. That's why it's called **identifier resolution** (标识解析), and why it's two words, not one: **identifier encoding** (标识编码) plus **identifier resolution** (标识解析). The encoding is the five-segment name — country, manufacturer, device, city, serial number. The resolution is the lookup that fetches what that name means.

| | Internet computer | Industrial thing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | Domain: `www.baidu.com` | Identifier: `86.10000/ac.qd.1024` |
| Resolver | DNS | Identifier resolution system |
| Returns | IP: `123.125.114.114` | IP + the business info attached to the identity |

Then the second decision: what does the identifier live *on*? Two kinds of carrier (标识载体). **Passive carriers** (被动标识载体) — barcode, QR code, RFID, NFC — hold a number but can't speak for themselves; they wait for a scanner. **Active carriers** (主动标识载体) — a UICC card, a communication module, an MCU, a terminal — carry their own power, network, and compute, so they can report on their own. The customer's site, where nobody will ever walk over with a scanner, is exactly the place you need the active kind — and it's the hard kind, because now you must supply power, connectivity, and security for every label.

A barcode got you a name. It didn't get you an answer. That's the first thing that broke.

## Whose Number, Whose Map?

So you build a resolution server. Yours knows your identifiers. The problem arrives when the customer's ledger says "X-3321" and your server has never heard of "X-3321" — because the customer runs their own server, with their own numbers, and the two don't meet.

This is not a bug in your code. It's the structural problem the whole industry has been stuck on. Look at it top-down:

| Level | The pain |
| --- | --- |
| Nation | No unified layout — every industry and region builds its own silo |
| International | Root nodes sit abroad — data sovereignty isn't yours |
| Between enterprises | Cooperating firms' data won't line up |
| Inside one enterprise | ERP, SCADA, PLC, history databases each keep their own world |
| On the shop floor | Part, in-process, and product identifiers all use different schemes |

One factory can't fix the top two rows by itself. So the resolution system is built as a shared, hierarchical tree, the way DNS has a global root. Five levels of node (标识解析节点五级): **international root node** (国际根节点) → **national top-level node** (国家顶级节点) → **secondary node** (二级节点, per industry or region) → **enterprise node** (企业节点) → **public recursive node** (公共递归节点). A request walks up and down this tree like a DNS query — except that at the end, instead of just an address, it hands back the records bound to the thing. (Don't confuse these five *levels* with the four *layers* of the resolution architecture — encoding → resolution → data → application: the five levels say *who forwards the query*, the four layers say *what the system is made of*.)

Three sentences govern the whole build: **nodes are the foundation, applications are the purpose, technology is the guarantee** (节点是基础、应用是目的、技术是保障). Lay the nodes first, let applications pull demand, let technology backstop the rest. One root family worth knowing is **Handle**, whose spirit is *equal, negotiated, co-managed* — no single master (GHR is the global root registry, ARS the auxiliary roots, LHS the lower-level authorized nodes).

And the payoff gets compressed into six promises, the six "one-code" slogans (六个一码): **one code = one thing** (一码一物), **one code = onto the cloud** (一码上云), **one code = full view** (一码全览), **one code = full collaboration** (一码协同), **one code = graded authority** (一码分权), **one code = ten thousand functions** (一码万能). From a single part up to a service ecosystem, one identity threads through all of it.

The part is now traceable on paper. But an identity is only worth something if the thing carrying it can *talk*. Remember Chapter 1's five problems — fast, lost, accurate, alive, talking? We bought off fast, lost, and accurate with the fieldbus. "Alive" and "talking" have been sitting on the books ever since. Time to build the pipes.

## Two Factories, Two Worlds

Your network has to cover two places that have almost nothing in common. Inside the factory, you own every wire and can demand millisecond determinism. Outside, there are 800 customer sites, your own branch, your suppliers, and the cloud — none of which you control. One design can't serve both, so you draw two.

That's the top cut of the network connection framework (网络连接框架). It has three jobs — **network interconnection** (网络互联, connect), **data interoperability** (数据互通, understand), and **identifier resolution** (标识解析, locate) — and it splits horizontally into **factory intranet** (工厂内网) and **factory extranet** (工厂外网).

Inside, the intranet is "two layers, three levels" (两层三级). The two layers are **IT** (information technology — the office, the enterprise) and **OT** (operational technology — the shop floor, the machines). The three levels are **enterprise, workshop, field** (企业级/车间级/现场级): IT owns the enterprise level (ERP, MES, SCM, CRM, the data center); OT owns the workshop level (industrial Ethernet, HMI, SCADA) and the field level (PLC, DCS, fieldbus, the machines themselves). Cut by *business* instead of by floor, the same intranet is four nets: the **production network** (生产网), the **office network** (办公网), the **security network** (安防网), and the **enterprise data center** (企业数据中心).

Outside, the extranet is "three dedicated lines plus one connection" (三个专线 + 一个连接): the **internet access line** (上网专线, OTN / MPLS VPN) ties the factory to the internet; the **interconnection line** (互联专线, SD-WAN) ties it to branches and suppliers; the **cloud access line** (上云专线, Cloud VPN) ties it to the industrial cloud; and the lone **connection** (上网连接, LTE / 5G / NB-IoT) reaches the machines already sold to customers. The first three are dedicated because both ends are organizations that need bandwidth and isolation; the last is a connection because the far end is a product scattered across a thousand workshops, reachable only over public wireless.

Now every box has a pipe. But "connected" is not "understood." Turn the power on and you hear the real problem: the PLC speaks Profinet, the old sensor mumbles Modbus over RS-485, the AGV chatters over wireless, and nobody translates.

## A Tower of Babel

This is Chapter 1's "talking" problem made concrete. The shop floor was never designed to be one language — it grew one protocol per generation of equipment. The full family splits first by medium, then into three: **fieldbus** (现场总线, the oldest wired dialect), **industrial Ethernet** (工业以太网, Ethernet hardened for the factory), and **industrial wireless** (工业无线网络).

- Fieldbus — eight names you'll meet: ProfiBus, ModBus, DeviceNET, CANOpen, CC-Link, AS-I, InterBus, ControlNet. Modbus matters most, because it was everyone's "common language" for years.
- Industrial Ethernet — four: **Profinet, EtherNet/IP, EtherCAT, ModBus TCP**. The same Ethernet frames as the office, tuned for real time.
- Wireless — eight more: WLAN, Bluetooth/BLE, WIA-PA/WIA-FA, LoRa, WirelessHART, ZigBee, ISA100.11a, 6LoWPAN.

You don't rip any of this out — the machines are paid for. You translate. Three ways (异构协议互通): **gateways and middleware** on the box, **protocol adapters and conversion software** in the middle, and **protocol data-format conversion** at the data level. The trick that makes it work: a gateway listens *south* to whatever the device speaks, converts the format in the middle, and speaks one unified model *north* — usually **OPC UA** or **MQTT** — to everyone upstream. South, many dialects; north, one.

Everything can talk now. But "talk" isn't "talk *on time*." Three loose ends from the sheet are still dangling.

## Three Tools for "On Time"

The first loose end is distance. The customer's site is 800 km away, and the sheet promised remote visibility and a 4-hour on-site response. A wired fieldbus can't reach it; only wireless can. That's **5G** (第五代移动通信), remembered by five numbers: one million connections per square kilometre, one gigabit per second, one millisecond latency, 99.9999% reliability, one microsecond of jitter. Three technologies make it real — high-frequency + multi-antenna, **network slicing** (网络切片), and **edge computing** (边缘计算) — and five applications it unlocks: remote collaboration, intelligent production, product-as-a-service, refined unmanned control, and real-time supply-demand matching.

The second loose end is determinism. Even inside the factory, ordinary Ethernet is best-effort: packets queue, collide, and retry, so the delay is *unpredictable*. A spindle servo can't wait for a router to feel like forwarding. That's **time-sensitive networking** (TSN, 时间敏感网络): a set of IEEE 802.1 Ethernet extensions that deliver **deterministic communication**. Read it as four old diseases and four cures:

| Traditional Ethernet's problem | TSN's mechanism | You get |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Poor real-time behaviour | Time synchronization (时间同步) | On-time delivery |
| Data jitter | Traffic control (流量控制) | Reliable data |
| Transmission collisions | Redundant recovery (冗余恢复) | Availability |
| Resource contention | Priority management (优先级管理) | Precision |

Strip it to its core and TSN is two things: one **global clock**, and one **transmission scheduler** that admits each flow by a timetable. "When will this frame arrive" stops being a gamble and becomes a number you can compute.

The third loose end is distance again, but of the data. With 800 machines streaming, shipping every byte to a distant cloud is slow, expensive, and none of the cloud's business — a spindle alarm should trip the local stop *now*, not after a round trip to Shanghai. That's **edge computing** (边缘计算): an open, distributed platform that fuses **network, compute, storage, and application** capability *next to* the data source and delivers intelligence *nearby*. Its entry device is the **edge gateway** (边缘计算网关). The division of labour with the cloud (边云协同) is the spinal reflex versus the brain: the edge handles real-time, short-cycle decisions and feeds summaries up; the cloud handles long-cycle, whole-fleet analysis and pushes models back down.

## Wrapping Up

Three loose ends, three tools, and the network layer finally stands: **connect** (interconnection, intranet and extranet), **understand** (protocols translated to one model), **locate** (identifier and resolution). Data now flows from 800 machines to the cloud in a steady, trustworthy stream.

And there we hit the wall we've been ignoring. The cloud is full of data, and the sheet hasn't shrunk by a word: the OEE must be *mutually agreed*, the trace must hand over machine, batch, and tool within 48 hours. Data sitting in a bucket is not a service. Someone has to turn that stream into something the customer will sign. That's the platform's job — and it's the next chapter.
