# Chapter 2 · One Blueprint

Last chapter closed with a floor full of parts: sensing, networking, cloud, big data, security. We decide to stop daydreaming and try something real first — land the sheet's "48-hour traceability," once.

## A Part You Can't Trace

You pull last month's part out of the warehouse and try to reconstruct its whole life from start to finish: which machine, which batch, which tool, what time the stock went down.

It stalls at step one: the names don't match.

- In the acquisition system, the machine is "Machine-07";
- In the ERP, it's "A07 machining center";
- In the customer's asset ledger, it's "X-3321".
- The tool is worse: the MES records "T-88", but the machine's own log says "tool slot 3".

One thing, three names in three systems. You hold all three records in your hands, yet you can't stitch a single line — missing is one shared "who is who" thread. "48-hour traceability," and you can't even swear "was this part made on Machine-07?"

That knocks you awake: every part so far was built in its own silo, and nobody ever thought they'd need to line up on one drawing. The problem isn't any one block; it's the *whole*. What we're missing isn't more parts — it's a drawing that says what it's made of and how the pieces cooperate.

## First Get It Straight: How Many Big Things?

Don't rush to draw lines. You spread the pile out and find it's only ever three big things:

- **Network**: connect the devices, make the data flow — the foundation;
- **Data**: once connected, every bit of value is driven by it — the engine;
- **Security**: keep the first two from blowing up — the insurance.

These are the three cores: **network, data, security**. Network is the supporting base, data is the core driver, security is the essential guarantee. Following these three, you also layer the optimization: how to optimize around **one machine**, around **one production line**, around **one enterprise** — three closed loops scaling from a single device all the way up to business.

But as you write, it starts to itch: data is tangled with the other two. The network carries data, security protects data, and data itself is the core — all three circle around "data," and the responsibilities won't separate.

So you revise: pull out a standalone **platform** (平台) to take on the integration, management, and modeling of data. Now each of the three words owns its own patch: **network owns "connect," platform owns "compute," security owns "protect."** This is the key cut from 1.0 to 2.0 — replacing *data* with *platform*.

Looking back at the road, you've actually climbed three steps: **digitalization** (数字化) (first, single-point optimization — understand one machine) → **networking** (网络化) (then, full interconnection — connect the whole plant) → **intelligence** (智能化) (finally, global intelligence — let data decide for you).

Three big systems aren't enough; you also need a *method* for "how to design," or you're drawing lines on a whim again. The method is three questions:

1. **Do what?** — the business view (goals, direction, scenarios, capabilities);
2. **Need what capabilities?** — the functional architecture (core functions, principles, elements);
3. **Build how?** — the implementation framework (layer structure, hardware/software, deployment).

Business goal first, then functions, then landing. Reverse the order and you get to tear it all down and start again.

## Business View: Whose Side Are You Speaking For?

"Do what" isn't something the one word "digitalization" can cover, either. You zoom out and back in, and find four levels to look at:

- **Industry layer**: macro — how a whole industry goes through digital transformation;
- **Business layer**: micro — a single enterprise's business and business model;
- **Application layer**: micro — which application scenarios it lands in;
- **Capability layer**: micro — which digital capabilities those scenarios need.

Only the industry layer is macro; the other three are all the enterprise's micro view.

## Functional Architecture: A "4 Layers × 3 Systems" Matrix

"What capabilities" is the chapter's heavyweight. First, one sentence as the through-line — the **core functional principle** of the industrial Internet is to fully interconnect and deeply coordinate the physical system and the digital space, then use intelligent analysis for decision and optimization. In plain words: build the real factory a digital shadow that mirrors it in real time, let the two sides talk, then use the data to tune the real factory back.

With the through-line set, expand it into three functional views. The neat part: you've actually seen all three before — they're the parts gathered last chapter, each taking its place.

**The network system = network interconnection + data interoperability + identifier resolution.**

- **Network interconnection** (网络互联): connect the elements, carry the transmission — last chapter's fieldbus, 5G, and LPWAN all belong here;
- **Data interoperability** (数据互通): make the elements "understand" each other — the "talking" problem (formats differ) we put on the books last chapter is this one's job, unifying the information model through protocols like OPC UA and MQTT;
- **Identifier resolution** (标识解析): give elements a "registered identity" — mark, manage, locate. Remember that untraceable part from the start? "Machine-07", "A07 machining center", "X-3321" — three names, and the missing step is exactly this: give every thing one unique, system-wide recognized identifier, so whoever looks it up finds the same "it."

One sentence to remember the order: **connect → understand → locate.**

**The platform system = edge layer + PaaS layer + application layer.**

- **Edge layer** (边缘层): do access, conversion, preprocessing, and edge analytics closest to the devices;
- **PaaS layer**: resource management, data & model management, modeling & analysis, application innovation;
- **Application layer**: industrial applications, developer community, app store.

Against last chapter's cloud computing: the edge layer does the "compute once, right nearby" job, the PaaS layer is the cloud's platform tier, and the application layer is the finally delivered function.

**The security system = five securities: reliability, confidentiality, integrity, availability, privacy & data protection.**

Versus the Internet's classic CIA triad (confidentiality, integrity, availability), two were added — **reliability** (industrial systems can't go down) and **privacy & data protection**. Those two additions are exactly the watershed between "factory" and "Internet" security demands.

Stack the three views together, then slice vertically into four layers — **device layer, factory layer, enterprise layer, industry layer** — and you get the "4 layers × 3 systems" master diagram. If you can redraw it from memory, you've got this chapter's skeleton.

## Implementation Framework: Four Buildable Systems

"How to build" is actually the least work: land the capabilities on the ground and they're four buildable systems — **network system, identifier system, platform system, security system**.

Note it's "identifier system," not "data system": at the landing step, identifier resolution gets pulled out as its own buildable project — because it's the root of that "names don't match" problem from the start. From blueprint to construction drawing, this is the last step.

## Wrapping Up

Back to that part from the start. With this blueprint, its fate is clear:

- Three names that don't match → **identifier resolution** gives it one system-wide unique identity;
- Three records in different formats → **data interoperability** unifies the wording;
- Where the data gets processed, how OEE is computed → all inside the **platform**;
- Keep the whole chain from being hacked → **security** holds the floor.

One sentence: network is the foundation, platform is the core, security is the guarantee; first decide what to do, then what capabilities you need, and only last how to build. The specific networks, platforms, and security — how each is actually built — is the business of the chapters to come.
