# Chapter 1 · One Order

We're digitizing a machine-tool factory: three people, a budget of 800,000 yuan a year. The plant ships 800 machining centers a year, has 800 of them installed at customer sites, 90 after-sales staff, and an 18% gross margin on whole machines.

## 1.1 Four Requirements on One Sheet

October 2024. An auto-parts maker shows up to audit us, because the three machining centers we supply run on their line. They hand us a sheet:

| Clause | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Connectivity rate | At least 90%, with remote visibility into running status |
| Traceability | Within 48 hours of an OEM trace request, hand over the machine, batch, and tool behind the part |
| Response | A person on site within 4 hours of unplanned downtime; 5,000 yuan/hour after that |
| Reporting | Monthly OEE, **mutually agreed** |

The first three are technical conditions. The fourth is a commercial one. "Mutually agreed" means: a number that *you* produce doesn't count.

Look closely at our factory. After-sales records live in the engineers' WeChat group. The "cause of downtime" field usually says "customer misoperation" or "to be investigated." You have no continuous record, and no single definition, of how your machines are doing at the customer's site. How many times the three machines stopped last year, and for how long — nobody has a clue.

And the trouble doesn't stop there: across systems (the ERP, the machine tools, and the tool ledger each keep their own copy), across sites (the machines sit in the customer's workshop), across regions (the customer is in another city).

Fine — now it's time to start losing hair. We don't just need to collect the scattered data living in chat records; we need that data to be *trustworthy*. Its source and its path must both be traceable.

But engineers have to fix things, right?

## The Obvious Idea

As an engineer — even one who coasts most days — you at least have some intuition. The core problem is, first, automatic data acquisition, and second, gathering that data somewhere. Without those two, no downstream processing is possible.

To acquire data you need sensors, and we have every kind — vibration, temperature, you name it — more than enough for our scenarios. The real problem now is getting the sensor data to *some device*. Otherwise we've collected it for nothing.

You pull a dusty old Raspberry Pi out of nowhere and cable every sensor into it over a data cable — USB, say. Nice! It's working. Data streams from the sensors into the Pi. Looks like we've cracked it. Sleep well tonight.

Really?

That night, while you're dead asleep, your on-call colleague wakes you up: some genius kicked the cable on his way past, the sensor dropped off the Pi, and half a night of data is gone. You storm to the factory, plug the cable back in, tape it down, scrawl a big warning sign, and go home for the second half of your sleep.

Nothing happens for a while. You keep wiring more sensors into the Pi, and then...

The Pi's storage fills up with non-stop sensor data, and the factory is now wallpapered with "don't kick the cable" signs. You also notice some data is out of order and the timestamps don't line up — the machines farther away always seem to lag a little. Sometimes a sensor fails and you have no idea; you end up patrolling the sensors every day like a security guard.

Right. The problem isn't solved. What next?

## An Improvement

As a 21st-century engineer you're no stranger to networks. When you watch a live stream, the streamer on the other side of the ocean isn't exactly lagging. That looks promising: we can kill the cables, maybe the latency too.

Cheapest change first: swap USB for wireless. The Pi gets a wireless adapter, the sensors hang off it, and data rides the factory's wireless network.

The flying cables are gone, and the data timing looks a bit better. But you're still patrolling like a guard, because when no data arrives you can't tell whether the sensor died, the line is under maintenance, or something else.

We're engineers, not guards — patrolling isn't the job. You have a bright idea: give each sensor a unique identity and let it report its own status, or have the system periodically ping it. Now we can sit back and watch a cyber-monitor. (The guard does that too, by the way.) And since you're already at it, why start every device by hand? Let the system manage acquisition start/stop itself.

You think this is it. Three days later you realize it isn't: every day from 9 to 11 a.m., half the data never arrives. Those two hours the workshop is working, cranes are moving, and the steel structure is shredding the signal.

So switch routes: drop in a 4G module and go through the carrier. The signal is better, but a card costs 20 yuan a month — 60 devices is 1,200, 14,000 a year. Finance is screaming.

And the disconnects got worse. When the network drops, the Pi goes amnesiac; when it comes back it resumes from the break point, and the gap in between stays blank forever. The customer asks "what was this machine doing from 10 to 11 last night?", and you can only answer: no idea.

Our system runs, and it collects data, but it isn't *reliable* — and the factory floor is a complex environment full of disturbances. We have to make the system reliable.

| Problem | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Fast enough? | Does data arrive on time? |
| Lost? | After a disconnect, can you backfill? |
| Accurate? | Is the timestamp right when it arrives — far machines always lag a little |
| Alive? | Do you know whether a sensor is alive or dead? |
| Talking? | Different vendors' formats — how do they become one? |

None of these five is the Pi's fault. They're the difficulty of the thing itself — "putting a machine online."

Congratulations. You've just invented the Internet of Things (物联网).

Take it apart and it's three layers: the **perception layer** (感知层) — your sensors, doing acquisition and recognition; the **network layer** (网络层) — the wireless and 4G you swapped in, doing transmission; the **application layer** (应用层) — doing processing and decisions. The three words map to its three traits: **comprehensive perception, reliable transmission, intelligent processing**. You thrashed around and only ever touched the first two layers; the third is still empty — that's for later.

But between "invented" and "usable" sits a whole factory.

## Networking: Don't Fight the Machine's Nervous System with a Raspberry Pi

Those five problems are all "connection" problems. One night you stare at the machining center's electrical cabinet and feel your face go red: inside, a wire has always connected the PLC (programmable logic controller) and the servo drives — tool changes, interpolation, spindle speed, all of it back and forth in milliseconds. The machine already has a nervous system, and you hung sensors on the outside and "tapped" the data with a Pi. Isn't that like giving crutches to someone who can walk?

You pull the Pi out and wire acquisition into the machine's own network. That wire is a fieldbus (现场总线) or industrial Ethernet (工业以太网). They were built for the shop floor: bidirectional, serial, multi-node, millisecond response, and built to survive oil, vibration, and electromagnetic interference. Three of your five problems — fast, lost, accurate — are largely what they were born to solve.

Here's the first thing you understand: industrial networking is not office Wi-Fi.

- **Fieldbus**: on the production floor, bidirectional, serial, multi-node communication between field devices, and between field devices and controllers. Two signature tricks — pushing control logic out into each instrument (**distributed function blocks** (分散功能块), the control station becomes a virtual concept); and letting instruments draw power straight off the communication line (**bus-powered** (通信线供电)), so hazardous areas need no separate power run.
- **Industrial Ethernet**: standard Ethernet, tuned for industrial environments. Four mainstream protocols — Modbus TCP/IP, EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP, Profinet — differing mainly in who owns real-time and who owns compatibility.

But wired, however stable, can't cover three spots: the AGVs (automated guided vehicles) running all over the floor, the customer site eight hundred kilometers away, and the corners where you can't pull a cable at all. Wireless fills in:

- **5G**: high rate, low latency, massive connections. Remember three name cards — uRLLC handles low latency (autonomous driving), eMBB handles high bandwidth (telemedicine), mMTC handles massive connectivity (smart factories).
- **LPWAN** (low-power wide-area network): LoRa, NB-IoT and the like, for sensors that "change a battery once a year and push a few numbers" — wide coverage, super-long battery life.
- **Indoor positioning** (室内定位): find machines, find people. UWB (ultra-wideband) is the most precise (0.1–0.5 m); RFID (radio-frequency identification) the crudest (10–100 m).

Look back at the sheet: 90% connectivity depends on these networks actually holding; 4-hour response depends on positioning (where the person is, where the machine is) plus communication (dispatch the nearest engineer). The "connection" hurdle is cleared.

As for the two remaining problems — "talking" (formats differ) and "alive" (do you know if a sensor is dead) — put them on the books. A faster network won't solve them. They'll wait for a bigger picture.

## Where Does the Data Go?

Data's now pouring in, and you hit a new problem: can't store it, can't crunch it.

800 machines, high-frequency sampling — a year of it is an astronomical number, and the Pi's disk is long gone. Worse, the OEE the customer wants is computed *across* 800 machines and *across* 800 customer sites — you can't just line up a hundred servers in a warehouse. Never mind the 800k budget; merely hiring people to run a machine room would eat you alive. And servers are the most awkward thing: idle all month, then slammed on report day.

You remember the cloud-drive subscription you pay every month. Why can't compute be rented by usage the same way? — that's **cloud computing** (云计算). Pile compute and storage into a resource pool, buy what you use, billed like water, gas, and electricity. By "how high up you rent," there are three tiers:

- **IaaS** (infrastructure as a service): rent bare machines and disks, install everything above the OS yourself — the most control.
- **PaaS** (platform as a service): rent a runtime environment; it manages scaling and fault tolerance for you — you relax, but you write to its rules.
- **SaaS** (software as a service): rent a ready-made function, works out of the box — the least effort, the least choice.

Cloud computing's real edge is cost: resource pooling, elastic scaling, on-demand self-service, metering. It turns "buy servers" into "buy service," and quietly hides away the differences between hardware brands and models.

## Stored Data Doesn't Turn into Money by Itself

You've rented the cloud and the data is lying in it, but the sheet's requirements haven't shrunk by a word: the OEE report must be "mutually agreed," traceability must hand over machine, batch, and tool within 48 hours. Data is dead; someone has to *use* it. This step is **industrial big data** (工业大数据).

First, where the data comes from — three classes:

- **Enterprise informatization data**: business data accumulated in the ERP, PLM (product lifecycle management), SCM (supply chain management), and CRM (customer relationship management) — the enterprise's traditional data assets.
- **Industrial IoT data**: sensors, logs, and monitoring on the machines, streaming through the MES (manufacturing execution system) in real time. Once smart equipment arrives, this class grows fastest.
- **External cross-domain data**: usage data after the product is sold, customer and supplier rosters, and the market outside.

With data in hand you still have to run a pipeline: **acquire** (pulled from the machines in real time via PLC, SCADA, and DCS) → **store & manage** (tackling "multi-source, heterogeneous": data from different sources and structures — relational, time-series, graph, key-value, everything) → **analyze** (time-series analysis for fault prediction, knowledge graphs (知识图谱) to capture the old masters' experience, multi-source fusion for price prediction and traceability).

Abstract that pipeline up one more level and it's a four-layer architecture: **acquisition & exchange layer → integration & processing layer → modeling & analysis layer → decision & control layer**. Fancy names. Translated: "pull it in → clean it and store it → build models and compute → take the conclusion and go to work."

Back to the sheet: 48-hour traceability means matching the machine, batch, and tool records together in the analysis stage; a mutually-agreed OEE means making the modeling-and-analysis result trustworthy enough for the customer to sign.

## Everything's Connected — Then It Gets Hacked

Just as you're breathing easy about the connectivity rate, a peer gets hit: ransomware crawled in through a networked machine and locked the whole production line via the internal network. 800 machines stop at once, every one of them "unplanned downtime," every one billed at 5,000 yuan per 4-hour block.

Only now do you realize: a 90% connectivity rate means you've opened a door to the outside on every machine. The deeper you connect, the more doors. **Security** is the leg you should have been building from day one and never got to.

Industrial Internet security risk, by target, falls into five classes: **device security, network security, data security, control security, management security**. The big difference from office network security: getting hacked in an office mostly loses data; getting hacked on a shop floor can hurt people. Control security, that one class, is wired straight to the physical world. Defenses come in three kinds: **industrial firewalls** (工业防火墙) (they read industrial protocols and block illegal access), **intrusion detection** (machine learning, data mining, and the like), and assorted newer detection techniques.

## Closing Time: A Floor Full of Parts

From the Raspberry Pi to now, we've gathered a floor full of parts: sensing, networking, cloud, big data, security. Each one holds up on its own, but nobody ever tried them together. Does the machine data reaching the cloud match the ledger in the ERP? Can three records stitch together one trace? Nobody knows.

The parts are all here. What's missing is a blueprint that says who is who, who comes first, and how they interlock.
