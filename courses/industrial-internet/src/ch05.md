# Chapter 5 · One Shield

That phone call was no drill. A peer's factory got hit by ransomware that crawled in through one networked controller and locked everything downstream of it. Every machine down is "unplanned downtime," and on our sheet that's 5,000 yuan an hour, compounding. We spent four chapters opening doors — 90% connectivity, remote visibility, the customer site on 5G, the cloud, the platform. Now we have to build the third leg of the blueprint, security, and we have to build it while every one of those doors stays open.

## The Worm That Reached Through the Wall

First, understand what we're actually afraid of. In 2010 a worm called **Stuxnet** (震网病毒) did something nobody had proven possible: it traveled through a network and physically wrecked machines. Its target was the uranium-enrichment centrifuges at Iran's Natanz plant. It slipped in on a USB stick, hunted for Siemens controllers, and rewrote their logic so the centrifuges spun themselves to destruction — while the operators' screens kept replaying normal numbers.

That last part is the lesson that matters. The attack didn't just break in; it *lied to the humans watching*. The course calls Stuxnet the **first destructive worm aimed at industrial control systems**, and the **first worm in history to attack real-world infrastructure**. The takeaway is one sentence: **a network attack can cause physical damage in the real world.**

Its six steps show why it worked:

1. **Infect** — ride a USB stick past the air gap onto a Windows host;
2. **Scan** — find the engineering stations and PLCs running Siemens software;
3. **Update** — phone home to a command server for fresh code so antivirus can't kill it;
4. **Destroy** — push tampered logic into the PLC so the centrifuges run wrong;
5. **Control** — hide, persist, hold the system;
6. **Deceive and demolish** — replay normal data to the monitors while the machines tear themselves apart.

Three conclusions fall out of it, and each rewrites a naive assumption: **an air gap is not security** (the USB stick *was* the gap); **the safety mechanisms are weak** (the PLC trusted whatever its controller told it); **a single defense always breaks**. The air gap has two more side effects — patches can't get in, so holes stay open for years, and there's no monitoring, so you find out late.

## Five Doors, Four Floors

So what exactly are we defending? Not one thing — five. The course lays them out as five parallel threats (五类安全威胁): **device, control, network, application, data** (设备/控制/网络/应用/数据).

| Threat | What it targets |
| --- | --- |
| **Device** (设备) | Robots, smart instruments, sensors on the floor |
| **Control** (控制) | Control software and control protocols |
| **Network** (网络) | The intranet, the extranet, and their borders |
| **Application** (应用) | The platform and industrial apps |
| **Data** (数据) | In-plant data, out-plant data, user information |

One attack usually crosses several at once: Stuxnet used a device-side entrance (the USB stick), a control-side weapon (tampered logic), and a data-side cover-up (replayed monitors). So the five aren't a checklist to work through one at a time; they're five faces of one surface.

To organize the defense, draw a grid — the security framework (安全框架). Horizontally, the five domains above, plus one column for **emerging technology** (人工智能、区块链、可信计算、零信任 — AI, blockchain, trusted computing, zero trust), which isn't a sixth domain but a set of tools that can reach into any of the five. Vertically, four layers: **industry, enterprise, factory, device** (产业层/企业层/工厂层/设备层). Horizontal means *which kind of security*; vertical means *which level of the world*. Confuse the two and you get nonsense like "put a firewall on the industry layer."

Now walk the five domains — each one changed in a specific way, and each change is what opened its door.

## Device: The Machine Learned to Think, So Now It Can Be Rewritten

The floor changed. A mechanical lathe could only be broken with a wrench; a CNC lathe runs an **embedded operating system + microprocessor + application software** (EOS + 微处理器 + 应用软件) — it's a computer now, and computers get rewritten. Four consequences: attacks **spread fast** (same firmware on a thousand machines = one exploit replicates everywhere); attacks **reach the device directly** (skipping the host above it); the **blast radius widens** (one device drags down a line, then a plant); and **vulnerability impact widens** (from one model to whole fleets of commodity devices).

Four defenses (设备安全防护):

- **Firmware attack-surface reduction** (FASR, 固件攻击面减少) — harden the OS kernel and protocol stack. Firmware (固件) is the special software stored *in the hardware* that bridges it to the OS above.
- **Vulnerability patching + patch management** (漏洞修复 + 补丁管理) — two jobs, one caution. The flow is *detect → download → test → approve → install*, and **test** and **approve** sit before **install** because a bad patch on a running line costs more than the vulnerability.
- **Hardware security enhancement** (硬件安全增强) — give the hardware an identity and use a secure chip or secure firmware as the **root of trust** (信任根).
- **Operation & maintenance control** (运维管控) — govern what gets plugged in. Stuxnet's USB stick is exactly what this blocks.

## Control: From a Locked Pyramid to an Open Floor

Control changed most of all: it went from **layered, closed, local** to **flat, open, global** (分层/封闭/局部 → 扁平/开放/全局). **IT and OT fused**, the control network opened up, and monitoring moved up while real-time control moved down. The old pyramid had middle layers that isolated things; the flat structure removed them.

This is the one domain where security and safety collide. **Information security** asks "is the system compromised?"; **functional safety** asks "will anyone get hurt?" — and the defenses fight each other: patching a PLC means stopping the line, a firewall adds latency, encryption slows real-time control. You can't do IT's "shut it down and fix it"; everything must stay online, non-interrupting, and reversible.

On the protocol side, five defenses (控制协议安全): **authentication** (身份认证 — knowledge-based "what you know", possession-based "what you have", intrinsic "what you are", combined); **whitelisting** (白名单 — default-deny, only known-trusted instructions travel); **access control** (访问控制 — after proving who you are, restrict what you may reach); **transport encryption** (传输加密 — a security layer in the stack, a security field in the message, or a tightened session); and **vulnerability testing** (脆弱性测试 — find the weaknesses systematically, where **fuzzing** (模糊测试) is 80% of the work because industrial protocols are binary and private, and only malformed inputs find the edges).

On the software side, four more: **tamper-proofing** (软件防篡改), **malware protection** (恶意软件防护), the classic **patching** (online, non-interrupting), and **security monitoring & audit** (安全监测与审计) — which leans on **unsupervised learning**, because a factory has no labeled attack samples (you can't experiment on a live line), only one class of "normal", so the algorithm learns normal and flags the strange.

## The Outer Rings: Network, Application, Data

The network changed from proprietary to open — IP everywhere, wireless everywhere, IT and OT touching, the private network meeting the internet. **Proprietary protocols** were their own shield (few tools, few experts); switching to **Ethernet/IP** let the whole IT attack toolkit walk in. Five defenses (网络安全防护): **network isolation** (网络隔离 — physical, protocol, application: three grades of loosening), **boundary control** (边界管控 — watch and block at the borders), **access management** (接入管理 — authenticate every device and identifier node), **transport protection** (通信和传输保护 — encryption and checksums), and **intrusion detection** (入侵检测系统, IDS — rule-based to catch known evil, anomaly-based, now deep learning, to catch unknown weirdness; industrial switches are weak, so a few hundred megabits of **DDoS** can drown one).

The application domain splits into **platform security** — six items: security audit, authentication & authorization, isolation between tenants, security monitoring, patching, **DDoS defense** — and **industrial app security** — five: code audit, patching, vulnerability detection, review & test, behavior monitoring & anomaly blocking.

Data is where the flow itself changed: it went from small, single-source, one-way to massive, multi-dimensional, **two-way** (少量单一单向 → 大量多维双向). Once data crosses the enterprise boundary, the question shifts from "will it leak" to "who is using it, where, and can we take it back." Defenses follow the data's position: **in transit, encrypt; in processing, identify sensitivity, authorize, desensitize; in storage, access control plus encryption**. On top of that, four emerging tools: **AI** (identify, trace, warn, control), **blockchain** (trustworthy storage and exchange), **trusted computing** (hardware + software guaranteeing the system runs as expected), and **zero trust** (零信任 — "never trust, always verify": no implicit trust even for the internal network).

## The Ledger Across the Wall

One emerging tool deserves its own look, because it solves a problem the others can't. Cross-enterprise collaboration has no shared center of trust — that's the whole reason data sharing is hard. **Blockchain** (区块链) is a **chained structure of blocks**: a **decentralized, tamper-proof distributed ledger**. Five traits: transparency, immutability (hash chains), decentralization, traceability, and lower transaction cost. In industry it means no single party owns the records, and every record can be audited back to its origin.

Industrial blockchain's four features line up as a chain — technical base (digital signatures, timestamps, distributed consensus, smart contracts) → functional edge (multi-party trust, cross-org collaboration, controllable sharing, auditable tamper-proofing) → application areas (trusted evidence, full-process traceability, asset supervision) → the conclusion that **blockchain and the industrial Internet must co-evolve** (链网协同).

Four industrial pains, four blockchain answers:

| The pain | The blockchain solution |
| --- | --- |
| Hard to build trust | **Distributed trust management** (分布式信任管理) — federated digital identities for people, machines, and things |
| Hard to confirm data ownership | **Data ownership & circulation management** (数据确权流转管理) — bind each asset to its identity tag on the ledger |
| Hard to share data safely | **Data security sharing** (数据安全共享管理) — homomorphic encryption lets models flow while raw data stays put |
| Hard to audit after the fact | **Event audit & traceability** (事件审计追溯管理) — every event on the chain, auditable back to its origin |

The memorable line from the sharing solution: *models move, data doesn't leave* (数据模型共享、数据不可见).

## Wrapping Up

Five domains, each defended at its own layer, with blockchain bridging the cross-enterprise gap. The shield is up: devices hardened, control authenticated, network isolated and monitored, apps and data guarded. The third leg of the blueprint stands.

And now, for the first time in this whole slog, we can stop building and look back. Because a question has been following us since the very first meeting, and we kept answering it with hardware instead of words. Procurement asked "does your machine support the industrial Internet?" We said "it has a 4G module," and he said *that doesn't count*. What *was* this thing we just spent five chapters building? What's it called, and what was it for? That's the last chapter.
